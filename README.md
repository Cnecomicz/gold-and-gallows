# Gold & Gallows

*Gold & Gallows* is a tabletop roleplaying game. You play with pen and paper,
dice, and (if you so choose) miniatures. It is a rules-light old-school 
dungeon/adventure game built around player judgment rather than character 
optimization. It was initially inspired by early-D&D (around the era of
OD&D, BECMI, and AD&D 1e and 2e), but it has many design decisions which
differ from D&D in interesting ways. At its core, *Gold & Gallows* attempts
to be streamlined, player-focused, and mechanically lethal (at least, if
played... uncreatively), all without requiring a large body of rules to
mediate every situation.

### Rules Booklet

[Gold & Gallows Rules Booklet](pdf/Gold%20&%20Gallows%20Rules%20Booklet.pdf)

The Rules Booklet is the core game. It contains the rules necessary to play
*Gold & Gallows*, including:

* character creation
* ability stats
* classes
* equipment
* weapons and armor
* adventuring gear
* consumables and usage dice
* hirelings
* property
* movement and initiative
* combat
* checks and saves
* advantage and disadvantage
* morale
* recovery and death
* magic and spells
* adversaries
* advancement
* treasure and related tables

The Rules Booklet is intended to be the primary rules reference. If you are
learning the game for the first time, start here. If you're intending only
to be a player, you can stay here, but it doesn't hurt to read the other
books too just to gain an appreciation for the game design and to better
understand how the game is expecting you to play.

### Referee Guidebook

[Gold & Gallows Referee Guidebook](pdf/Gold%20&%20Gallows%20Referee%20Guidebook.pdf)

The Referee Guidebook is about running the game rather than learning its
mechanics. *Gold & Gallows* intentionally leaves many situations outside
the scope of explicit rules. The Referee Guidebook explains why, and provides
principles for adjudicating those situations consistently. It discusses
topics including:

* the design philosophy of *Gold & Gallows*
* old-school approaches to play
* player skill and character skill
* making rulings
* information and investigation
* encounter design
* challenge and danger
* exploration
* time
* traps
* monsters
* NPCs and hirelings
* campaign structure
* common problems at the table
* advice for handling situations the rules do not explicitly cover

The Referee Guidebook is particularly important for new Referees, or Referees
who are accustomed to games where every conceivable action has a corresponding
rule or skill. For both Referees and players alike, the following is gospel:

**The absence of a rule is not an absence of an available action.**

When the rules do not provide an answer, the Referee should make a reasonable
ruling and keep the game moving. The Referee Guidebook ensures your headspace
aligns with the intended design, so that doing this becomes second nature.

### Modular Rules

[Gold & Gallows Modular Rules](pdf/Gold%20&%20Gallows%20Modular%20Rules.pdf)

The Modular Rules are a collection of optional mechanics that can be added
to a *Gold & Gallows* campaign when the Referee wants more structure,
additional procedures, or a different treatment of a particular situation.
These rules were generally omitted from the core rules because they are:

* more mechanically involved,
* useful only in particular circumstances,
* alternatives to existing mechanics,
* and/or simply outside the narrow requirements of the core ruleset.

The Modular Rules include systems covering areas such as:

* alternate dice mechanics
* time and random encounters
* chases
* corruption
* alternate death and injury systems
* alternate approaches to HP
* scars
* exploration
* social mechanics
* downtime
* domain and campaign play
* additional class and character mechanics
* and other specialized procedures

The modules are not intended to be used all at once, and in fact are
discouraged entirely for first time tables. Any module's inclusion should
be a carefully considered decision. Most are mutually incompatible. Many
replace core mechanics. Some are intentionally experimental. If any modular
rules are adopted, it should be only after Referees and players jointly
agree on which ones suit their campaign and table.

Also, the modules aren't just optional expansions; they're also examples
of how the Referee can think about rulings. A number of the modular rules
can be understood as examples of how a Referee might handle edge cases through
a consistent ruling. So rather than thinking of Modular Rules as just an
expansion book to the core rules, you can also consider them as optional
crystallized procedures of Referee judgments.

## Overview of gameplay and mechanics

At the table, one person takes the role of Referee, directing the flow of
gameplay and adjudicating outcomes. Everyone else comprises the players,
who control typically one character each. The Referee describes the situations
and entities that the characters encounter, the players describe what their
characters attempt to do, and the Referee describes the results, calling
for dice if the outcome is uncertain. The loop then repeats. While this is
typical of many pen and paper TTRPGs, *Gold & Gallows* differs in how its
specific mechanics incentivize particular gameplay choices.

Characters are not defined by long lists of skills or tightly prescribed
options. Instead, players are expected to interact directly with the fictional
world: asking questions, making plans, exploiting their equipment, taking
risks, negotiating, scouting, and deciding when to fight or run. *Gold &
Gallows* requires good decisions instead of good character builds, and the
lightweight rules purposefully stay out of the players' way to keep the
table from getting bogged down in mechanics.

*Gold & Gallows* uses a small number of rules to cover a wide range of
situations. In particular, it doesn't even require any math to play (go
figure, huh?!), and the intention is that the rules get out of the way of
just having a good time. 

### Thread the Needle

When an action is opposed by an enemy, obstacle, or other value, roll a
d20 that lands above the opposing value and below your relevant ability
score. This single mechanic, called **Threading the Needle**, handles attacks,
saves, spellcasting, and most other opposed situations. It requires no
modifiers and thus no arithmetic at the table. The Referee can further dial
difficulty by granting Advantage or Disadvantage to a roll, depending on
the circumstances surrounding the situation.

### Roll under/over

For an unopposed check, roll a d20 strictly below, or sometimes above, the
relevant ability score.

### No skills

There is no list of skills that tells players what their characters can
attempt. If you want to search for a trap, deceive a guard, climb a wall,
negotiate with a monster, or investigate a strange object, describe what
your character actually does. The Referee determines what happens and whether
a roll is necessary.

### Gold is advancement

Characters level up by spending gold, so spoils/treasure are therefore not
just a reward for adventuring, but the means by which characters progress.
Characters must take risks to acquire wealth, then decide how much of that
wealth to invest in advancement, equipment, hirelings, property, and other
pursuits.

### Dangerous combat

Combat is not assumed to be an appropriate solution to every problem. Enemies
can be dangerous, encounters can be uneven, and retreat is a legitimate,
often intelligent, outcome. A successful character (and success is more
often than not measured by survival) has learned to ask, "How can we accomplish
this without fighting?" Since characters level up with gold, not experience
points, often there may be no mechanical advantage to picking a fight for
its own sake.

### A world that does not revolve around the characters

The world should not necessarily be balanced around the party. Some encounters
are too dangerous to challenge. Opportunities might disappear if the players
wait too long. A lot of problems can't be solved by rolling well. (If you're
rolling at all, especially without first accumulating some Advantage, it
might already be too late.) Players are expected to learn about the world,
make judgments about risk, and live with the consequences of their decisions.
Clever gameplay should always outweigh a mechanical check.

## Philosophy for players

*Gold & Gallows* works best when players approach problems as people in
a dangerous world, rather than as characters looking for the correct ability
on their character sheet.

### Describe what you do

Since *Gold & Gallows* does not have skills, rather than making Perception
checks, for example, you should ask questions and describe your character's
actions. For instance, "I inspect the floor around the statue for pressure
plates." The principle is that your decisions are made in the fiction of
the game world instead of along the mechanics available on your character
sheet. Once you have described your action, the Referee then tells you what
happens, incorporating dice rolls if the outcome is uncertain.

### Use the world

Your equipment, surroundings, allies, enemies, and information are all
potential tools. A ten-foot pole, a mirror, a rope, a torch, a sack of flour,
a hireling, or a clever lie will be more valuable than a favorable stat.
The probabilities for dice outcomes are, perhaps surprisingly, actually
often rather unfavorable to you, so success often means stacking the deck
in your favor through clever decision making, which may grant your character
Advantage, or sometimes may even guarantee your desired outcome, circumventing
any need to roll at all.

### Gather information before taking risks

You do not need to fight something merely because you encountered it. Since
*Gold & Gallows* does not have standard experience points, there's often
no "reason" to do so anyway. Good players will observe, investigate, scout,
negotiate, set traps, find another route, learn what an enemy can do, etc.
Information is one of the most valuable resources you can accumulate. 

### Do not expect encounters to be fair

The world is not a sequence of encounters calibrated to your current level;
it is a living and breathing place with consequences for your actions. You
might encounter something you cannot directly defeat, now or maybe ever.
Retreating is often a legitimate success; if you recover valuable treasure
and make it home alive, the expedition succeeded.

### Make decisions with incomplete information

The game is intended to create meaningful uncertainty. You won't always
know whether a door is safe, whether an enemy is bluffing, or whether the
treasure ahead is worth the danger. Make the best decision you can with
the information available. Gaining experience in this is probably just as
impactful as leveling up your character, if not more so.

### Remember that your character is not your character sheet

A low ability score does not prevent you from having a good idea, and a
high ability score doesn't save your ass from a bad or no plan. Your
character's stats do matter when all else fails and you're rolling dice,
but what you choose to do before you even get to that point matters more.

## Philosophy for Referees

The Referee's job is not to defeat the players, nor protect them from danger,
nor force them toward a predetermined story. The Referee presents a world,
adjudicates the consequences of player actions, and keeps that world moving.

### Make the world understandable

Players should be able to make informed decisions. Do not hide every important
fact behind a roll. (In fact, the opposite should probably be the norm:
think hard about whether *any* important fact should be gated to a roll.)
If careful observation would reveal something, tell the players directly.
If experimentation would reveal something, let them experiment. If it's
just something they ought to know, then you probably just need to tell them.
A failed roll should not be the only thing standing between the players
and basic information they could reasonably discover.

### Do not require a roll when the fiction already answers the question

If the players have a rope, enough time, and a plan for how to use it, they
should simply be allowed to. If they have discovered the secret door and
explain how they open it, do not roll just because "secret doors require
a check." Save rolling only for when uncertainty and consequences make the
outcome meaningful, and otherwise just allow outcomes to occur uncontested.

### The rules don't limit what you can do

*Gold & Gallows* is rules-light, so the following fact is particularly true,
but even in more mechanically dense rulesets: the rules cannot anticipate
every possible action. When they do not provide an answer, make a reasonable
ruling based on the circumstances and move on. If you don't remember a
mechanic, you're welcome to make a snap decision and double check your work
later. When the rules do cover something, don't let them prevent you from
keeping the game moving, but understand that changing a rule is different
from failing to remember one. Players need to know which parts of the game
are mechanically certain so that they can make informed decisions.

### Do not protect the players from consequences (good or bad)

The Referee should not adjust the world to ensure that the characters survive;
danger should be real. At the same time, the Referee should not deliberately
engineer character deaths. Present the situation honestly and let the players
decide what risks to take. This becomes fair and fun, as long as you are
adequately signposting that the risks are there.

### Do not balance the world around the party

As a flipside to something already stressed in the player philosophy section,
you don't have to ensure that encounters are appropriate for the characters'
current level or ability. Some things can be terrifying or impossible to
defeat directly, and the players should be able to discover this through
play and decide how to respond. Again, signposting this is critical; this
philosophy does not work if players are blindsided by forced outcomes or
cheap deaths.

### Reward cleverness

When players find a solution that makes a difficult problem trivial, ***THAT
IS A SUCCESS, NOT A PROBLEM!*** Do not add another obstacle just because
the players found an effective solution. You should reward preparation,
creativity, and understanding, which when done well will trivialize many
situations.

### Let the world respond

In order for the game to feel like a living world and not a series of
encounters, player actions should have consequences: enemies learn, factions
react, time passes, supplies run out, opportunities disappear, allies can
become enemies, enemies can become allies, etc.

## *Gold & Gallows*' implicit design

The rules are built around a particular gameplay loop: explore, investigate,
take risks, acquire treasure, escape, spend gold, become more capable, and
take greater risks. The rules are simple so that the decisions can be
complicated; notice that this macro-level loop does not depend on rules
or mechanics. This loop naturally encourages:

* exploration
* information gathering
* preparation
* clever use of equipment
* negotiation
* cooperation
* resource management
* choosing battles carefully
* retreating when appropriate
* accepting imperfect characters
* learning from previous expeditions

It naturally discourages:

* treating combat as the default solution
* relying on character-sheet abilities to solve problems
* assuming every encounter is balanced
* spending resources without considering consequences
* treating death as impossible
* expecting the Referee to provide a predetermined story
